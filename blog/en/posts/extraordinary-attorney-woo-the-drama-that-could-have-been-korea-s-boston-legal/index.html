Extraordinary Attorney Woo: The Drama That Could Have Been Korea's Boston Legal

First saved in Studio 2026-09-15 · Published 2026-09-18

Reflections

0. The drama that could have been a Korean Boston Legal

Maybe I just expected too much, but while Extraordinary Attorney Woo (the 2022 Korean drama) was fun, it also left me deeply disappointed. Through episode 8, I genuinely wondered whether we were finally getting a "Korean Boston Legal." Personally, I think episodes 1–8 were even better as a legal drama than Miss Hammurabi, another Korean legal drama. (The inquisitorial approach of Korean courts just isn't fun to watch...) But from episode 9 on, it became a show I kept watching only to see how much weirder it could get. If it had stuck with the solid legal-drama format of episodes 1–8, I suspect its ratings would have skyrocketed even further, like the Korean drama Stove League. It's a shame in many ways. Still, part of me thinks we should be grateful we got even this much.

In this post, I've organized my frustrations by topic.

1. Two dramas: Episodes 1–8 and 9–16

Episodes 9–16 are written so differently from episodes 1–8 that you'd almost think a different person wrote them. I believe this reflects the production company's or writer's intent to play it safe on ratings.

Episodes 1–8 experimented with "a Korean version of an American-style legal drama, with the genders flipped." Then, as a hedge in case that flopped, episodes 9–16 seem to have been stuffed with every cliché from the conventional Korean drama playbook. The strategy appears to have been: if the response to episodes 1–8 was lukewarm, keep ratings up with clichés from episode 9 onward. Honestly, I can't explain the drop-off any other way.

The problems I discuss below are all symptoms of this "second drama": the flattening of the legal reasoning, plot developments that lurch forward with no verisimilitude, and Kwon Min-woo becoming a one-dimensional character.

2. The law gets flattened

The most irritating thing about episodes 9–16 is that every legal issue that came up was flattened.

Episode 12: Whose mouth should deliver the reasoning behind the ruling? The judgment on the merits was at least delivered by the presiding judge. But wouldn't it have been better for the lawyers themselves to present the reasoning that leads to the ruling?

Episodes 13–14: The law of public property. The legal doctrine on managing cultural properties¹ became paper-thin. The law on how public property—especially property for public use, like roads—is managed and used is a recurring issue even in the 50-point case questions of the administrative law section of Korea's Grade 5 civil service exam. There's a substantial body of doctrine and case law on it. This topic allows for a debate among competing issues such as:

  • The legal nature of public property
  • Private autonomy, and its relationship with Article 4 of Korea's Road Act, which restricts the exercise of private rights over road land
  • Freedom of passage
  • The enhanced right of general use held by adjacent residents

But the drama reduced all this to an argument over "how far does a cultural property extend?" That isn't a legal issue; it's a general value judgment.

The way the ruling was reached is also disappointing. The episode was adapted from the dispute over cultural property viewing fees at Cheoneunsa Temple on Jirisan Mountain in South Korea, and the actual Korean court likewise ruled, in effect, that fees can't be collected from people who are merely passing along the road. So I'm not saying the conclusion itself was wrong. The problem is how it got there.

Cultural properties are also public property (for preservation). So it shouldn't have ended with the single argument that "the road is public property, so its use can't be obstructed." The drama should have shown the balancing of interests: which weighs more, the provincial road or the national treasure? The scope of preserving a cultural property can be read narrowly, as the head monk in the show does, as "preservation in the strictest sense," but it can also be approached from the angle of "enjoyment." Skipping that balancing altogether and just going "the provincial road is public property, so we win"... come on, that's too much. There must be plenty of interesting precedents out there.

Episodes 15–16: Attorney-client privilege (ACP). Nearly every legal drama features a case about ACP at some point. In American dramas, the common variations were:

  • The client is kept from confessing the crime, though the lawyer knows it in their gut.
  • The client is made to confess, and then killed off in the story.

These variations arise from the tension among the following doctrines under U.S. law:

  • Confessions of past crimes are protected by the privilege.
  • But a lawyer cannot knowingly present false testimony or evidence to the court (the duty of candor).
  • If the client seeks to use the lawyer's help for a future crime or fraud, the privilege does not apply (the crime-fraud exception).

Perhaps reflecting the fact that this doctrine was not codified in Korea when the show aired², Woo split the issue into three:

  • The lawyer's moral judgment
  • The separation of the corporation from the natural person
  • Conflicts of interest and breach of trust: to represent Raon, Hanbada must not represent the co-CEO. Underlying this is the co-CEO's liability to the company, i.e., breach of trust.

It was quite refreshing to see the show address the distinction between a corporation and a natural person. But I think failing to properly argue fiduciary duty or breach of trust was a mistake. Of course, Korea also has a director's duty of care as a good manager (Article 681 of the Korean Civil Act, applied via Article 382(2) of the Korean Commercial Act) and a duty of loyalty (Article 382-3 of the Korean Commercial Act). But because Korean court rulings turn on highly individualized, specific circumstances, it's hard to discuss these the way you would in the U.S. (Since Korea's 2025 Commercial Act amendment added shareholders to those to whom the duty of loyalty is owed, there might be more to say now.) Even so, handling attorney-client privilege this thoughtlessly?

From legal drama to detective story. The sudden shift in episode 14 to tracking down the owner of Haengbok Noodles seems to be a common mistake among legal drama writers: the legal drama quietly turns into a detective story. The Good Wife also took a lot of flak for having everything resolved by evidence Kalinda brought in. Well, the writer said this was their first legal drama. I don't like it, but I can let it slide.

3. How the show portrays big law firms and senior attorneys

Through episode 11, I half-wondered whether Hanbada was modeled not on Bae, Kim & Lee (Taepyeongyang), one of Korea's biggest firms, but on Jipyong, another Korean firm. Episode 12, at least, did a good job (shallow as it still was) of showing the ideals versus the realities of big-firm lawyers. But Jung Myung-seok, though senior, isn't a partner, so the show couldn't explore that issue as deeply as The Good Wife did.

The case in episodes 13–14 is a lawsuit to get back 3,000 won (about two U.S. dollars). It's not even a class action, so the very idea that such a big firm would take this case... How rich is that client, hiring four lawyers for a 3,000-won lawsuit?

It turns out Jung Myung-seok (as befits a senior attorney) seems to have run this case from the start with an eye to landing work from the Buddhist community. But do big law firms really use that Imperial Japan–style strategy of "slap them hard enough and they'll come to the table"? Does that actually work in Korea? I'm genuinely asking, because I don't know.

That said, the real case did end in a similar way. The Cheoneunsa dispute that inspired this episode was only resolved in 2019, after the lawsuit. Korean government bodies, including the Ministry of Environment, the Cultural Heritage Administration, and South Jeolla Province, signed an agreement with Cheoneunsa, providing various forms of support such as purchasing the provincial road land in exchange for the temple abolishing its viewing fee. Of course, that outcome came from the government stepping in, not from a law firm's strategy. Still, Jung Myung-seok's suggestion that "ultimately, this has to be negotiated with the state" wasn't far off from what actually happened.

4. Lurches without verisimilitude, and deus ex machina

Fine. Sudden plot swerves are understandable, as long as they feel believable. The problem is that they didn't.

Episode 13: An unexplained lurch. Woo Young-woo swerved so abruptly I almost wondered if she was Haruhi. The Melancholy of Haruhi Suzumiya at least had Kyon's narration to make Haruhi's impulsiveness convincing; Woo doesn't even offer that. The only scene in this episode where the writer paid attention to verisimilitude was the meeting with Jun-ho's sister and the reaction it provoked. If so, why drag in Jeju Island at all? For the dolphins? An episode I just can't make sense of.

The device that takes Jung Myung-seok down. When Jung Myung-seok coughed up blood at the end of episode 12, I wondered what it was foreshadowing. (Please don't get sick, best boss ever... ㅠㅠ) And at the end of episode 13, the writer once again used a deus ex machina to knock him down.

Episode 14: Jun-ho and Young-woo's breakup. Their breakup was far too abrupt. Sure, it was bound to happen at some point. But did it have to happen like this? I keep making the comparison, but in Boston Legal, Katie and Jerry Espenson didn't become a couple from the start either. There were several clashes between them before the series finale (season 5, episode 13) hinted that they'd gotten together. I think conflict of that scale can easily be handled within the office setting. Yet Woo treated it extremely melodramatically.

Contrived devices. The after-party at the end of episode 12 was somewhat contrived, though it worked well as a device for showing the connection with Ryu Jae-sook.

The finale also had some minor stuff that ticked me off, like Kim Chan-hong rambling about "developer spirit" and persuading some nerd to do his bidding. It didn't need to be Snowden-level, but it should have been more plausible. I mean, you could at least have worked Eric Raymond's The Cathedral and the Bazaar or Manuel Castells's The Internet Galaxy into a character like that... ㅠ_ㅠ

In an anthology format, conflicts don't have to be resolved. Legal dramas are episodic. In a well-made episodic show, conflicts don't need to be resolved into a synthesis or reach catharsis (in the Aristotelian sense), especially if the resolution comes through abrupt plot turns like these. Just think of one of the most celebrated episodes of Boston Legal: Alan's friend is charged with murder and acquitted, then confesses that he had in fact deceived Alan.

5. Kwon Min-woo, a flat character

Episode 12. Why turn Kwon Min-woo into such a thoughtless villain? He could easily have gone in a different direction, but the character became far too flat.

Episode 13. If they were going to have him share lodgings with Beardy's group, they should at least have paid off the foreshadowing between Dong Geu-ra-mi and Kwon Min-woo. Instead, a Choi Su-yeon/Kwon Min-woo connection came out of nowhere. They even mixed in Kwon Min-woo's family history, eliminating any room for the already-flat character to become three-dimensional "as a lawyer."

Episode 14. As everyone has pointed out, the Min-woo/Su-yeon storyline makes no sense. Then why did Beardy and Geu-ra-mi tag along? Just to squeeze in a karaoke scene? The way the show forced in their traveling together, I was completely sure it was setting up Geu-ra-mi and Min-woo.

The finale. And then that careless remark Kwon Min-woo made in front of Tae Su-mi... I just don't know anymore ㅠㅠ

In the end, Kwon Min-woo has a change of heart. But can this really be settled by a change of heart alone? That said, this ending follows a formula running through Korean dramas like SKY Castle and Hospital Playlist:

The middle class must be pure; past ambitions of upward mobility are bad, but must not be punished.

Woo just followed that formula faithfully, so I don't have much to say. Just as in SKY Castle it was Hye-na, not Ju-young, who bore the sin, Kwon Min-woo could become good only because Choi Su-yeon, the "spring sunshine," absolved him. It's the development I hate most, but it's also amusing, like an anagram of the K-drama formula.

6. Other forms of discrimination revealed behind Woo Young-woo

In fact, while putting Woo Young-woo front and center, the writer openly displays other forms of discrimination. One is academic pedigree. In the show, everyone from Seoul National University (Korea's most prestigious university) acts freely, while graduates of the fictional Hana University read the room or flatter others. The same goes for scenes where characters check each other's bon-gwan (ancestral clan seat) and family ties, or Bae In-cheol's absurd attempts at wining and dining.

In episode 9, kids from Daechi-dong, Seoul's elite cram-school district, are portrayed as "the pitiful people around me." The scenes above show which mechanisms of discrimination are most visible in this worldview. The Kwon Min-woo formula I described earlier—desiring upward mobility but escaping punishment—also grows out of this worldview.

7. What I still liked

Episode 12 was fun. Though it went in a different direction, it reminded me of the Boston Legal episode where a genuinely hard-to-communicate-with employee files a retaliation lawsuit under U.S. civil rights law.

Jung Myung-seok's ex-wife showing up in episode 14 wasn't very plausible, but it made sense given the characters. Yes, someone as much of a unicorn as Jung Myung-seok might have an ex-wife who comes to see him out of worry, even after divorce. I also liked the scene where, watching Jung Myung-seok come alive, she realizes how lonely she had been. That kind of scene is rare in Korean dramas, which made it even better. Please don't die, unicorn boss...

Episode 13 was weak as an episode, but Dong Geu-ra-mi's antics were worth watching. Without them, I'd have been even more disappointed.

I also enjoyed Choi Su-yeon's growth arc and actress Seo Hye-won's performance as Chae Da-hye. Including the child actor who played Kim Min-ji in episode 9, the supporting cast's acting was really excellent.

Closing

I doubt I'll watch season 2. But if it does come out, I hope it goes a step beyond episodes 1–8 and becomes a drama truly worthy of Boston Legal.

Notes

¹ In this post, I use the term "cultural property" (munhwajae), following the terminology in use when the show aired. Under Korean law, the term "cultural property" was replaced by the "national heritage" (gukga yusan) system in May 2024. In addition, from May 2023 a system of exemptions from viewing fees for state-designated cultural properties took effect, eliminating the fees previously charged at temples such as those of the Jogye Order, Korea's largest Buddhist order.

² On January 29, 2026, an amendment to Korea's Attorney-at-Law Act codifying ACP (newly adding Article 26-2) passed the National Assembly of Korea. The amended law takes effect one year after promulgation. It sets out exceptions for cases such as when the lawyer is an accomplice of the client, or when the client attempted to use the communications for illegal acts—in effect, introducing a mechanism similar to the crime-fraud exception.
