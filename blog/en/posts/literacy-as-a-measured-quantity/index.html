Literacy as a Measured Quantity

"She has high literacy" hides a question: high with respect to which texts?

First saved in Studio 2026-09-18 · Published 2026-09-18

Modeling Society

Literacy strikes me as an ill-defined word. No matter how educated someone is, how well they understand a text depends on what it's about and where it appears. I can read a piece of mathematical writing far better than the average middle schooler, but I doubt I could make more sense of an op.gg page1 than they could, or communicate any better with my teammates in a game of League of Legends.

So before assuming that a person has one global literacy, we might start with a local literacy, defined for each text.

Literacy with respect to a given text AA: the ability to understand AA and, on that basis, to judge or act.

A caveat is worth stating up front. Defined this way, local literacy takes in background knowledge along with vocabulary and grammar, so it isn't a pure linguistic ability isolated from everything else. But when we say in ordinary life that someone understands a text, we aren't stripping out background knowledge either. Asking how well a person actually handles a particular text is closer to the problem I want to take up.

"Understand" and "judge" are still vague. We can't measure them directly, but we can name a few proxies.

  1. Vocabulary: how much of the wording in AA the reader knows.
  2. Sentence comprehension: how accurately the reader follows the syntax, and structures like condition, cause, and contrast.
  3. Contrast: given information that conflicts with a claim in the text, whether the reader can work out how the two relate.
  4. Reconciliation: whether the reader can infer what further information would resolve that conflict.
  5. Action: whether the reader can work out what to do, given both the text and their own position and interests.

None of this is new. It overlaps a good deal with what the nonfiction passages on the Korean section of the CSAT2 set out to measure. Write l(x,A)l(x,A) for the local literacy reader xx shows on text AA. How many CSAT questions a student gets right on passage AA is one way to estimate it.

From local to global

Trouble starts when we try to fold ability across many texts into a single number. One person reads mathematics well and another reads legal documents well, so performance on any one text tells us nothing about how they compare in general. To build a global literacy we have to average local literacy over many texts, which means choosing a distribution PP to average over:

LP(x)=EA∼P[l(x,A)]L_P(x)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P}[l(x,A)]

Draw texts one at a time according to PP, measure how well the person handles each, take the mean. Change PP and the number changes with it.

That is what the model makes visible. Load the distribution with contracts and lawyers come out ahead; load it with writing built from one online community's vocabulary and that community's regulars do. So "she has high literacy" conceals a question: high with respect to which distribution of texts? The competing definitions of literacy turn out to be less disagreements about l(x,A)l(x,A) than different answers to that question — which PP, and which texts get weighted more heavily.

Candidate 1: treat every text alike

The obvious first move is to draw uniformly from all Korean texts, averaging local literacy across every Korean text there is. Call this statistical literacy.

Formally, if the set of texts A\mathcal{A} is finite and every text really can be treated alike, set

P(A)=1∣A∣P(A)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}

and LP(x)L_P(x) is the unweighted mean over all of them. But now we have to say what counts as one text. A single comment on DC Inside3 is one text, and so is a journal article. Count by sentences or by words instead and the proportions move again. A terms-of-service page is written once and read a million times, so we have to decide whether to count the writing or the readings, and whether texts nobody reads belong in the population at all. Counting by vocabulary runs into the same thing. Build a corpus out of all actual Korean usage and myeongjing and jikjo4 may well show up less often than aljjaltakkalsen or ㅇㄷㅊㅇ5.

So "draw uniformly" doesn't dissolve the problem. It defers it by one step. A uniform distribution needs a sample space before it can be defined at all, and building that sample space is already where the measurer's choices come in. Weights are not discovered but chosen.

Candidate 2: count the texts people actually meet

That problem came from leaving the unit of counting open. One way around it is to shift the population from texts that exist to occasions when someone actually reads one. Now the unit is fixed: one reading. A KakaoTalk message read once is one encounter, a lease agreement read once is another, a notice read by a million people is a million. Texts nobody reads fall out on their own. What we had to stipulate before is settled here by definition.

Each person then has their own distribution PxP_x over the texts they meet, and their literacy becomes

LPx(x)=EA∼Px[l(x,A)]L_{P_x}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P_x}[l(x,A)]

The idea is simple enough. Developers read code and technical documentation; lawyers read contracts and case law; League of Legends players see the game's abbreviations and tactical shorthand over and over. Average over what each person actually meets and you are using a different distribution for each of them. That is the cost of the fix: when the distributions differ, LPx(x)L_{P_x}(x) and LPy(y)L_{P_y}(y) can't be set side by side and compared.

Candidate 3: count what it costs to get it wrong

Counting encounters may not be enough either. Misreading a streamer's nickname and misreading a special clause in your lease are each one reading, and the consequences are nothing alike.

To capture that, attach a weight w(A)w(A) to each text.6 Weighted literacy is then

LP,w(x)=EA∼P[w(A) l(x,A)]EA∼P[w(A)]L_{P,w}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P}[w(A)\,l(x,A)]}{\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P}[w(A)]}

Set w(A)=1w(A)=1 and we are back to the plain average. Raise w(A)w(A) as the expected harm from misreading a text grows, and understanding that text counts for more. The quantity shifts from "how many texts does this person understand" toward "how well does this person understand the ones that matter."

Call this literacy as economic cost. If the point is to reduce what someone loses by misreading things in the course of ordinary life, it makes sense to weight contracts, terms of service, tax notices, medical instructions, and administrative rulings heavily. Not recognizing a streamer rarely costs you money or rights. Misreading a contract can cost you a great deal of both.

Which distribution does the CSAT use?

Count everything the average Korean reads in a year and very little of it looks like a CSAT nonfiction passage. The exam doesn't go after what people meet often; it goes after texts that demand the vocabulary, syntax, and inference thought necessary for academic work at university. It runs on a normative distribution PCSATP_{\mathrm{CSAT}} rather than an encounter distribution PxP_x. And as a college scholastic ability test, that is exactly what it should do. The trouble starts when the result leaves the exam hall and gets treated, without qualification, as a number for someone's literacy. What we have is LPCSAT(x)L_{P_{\mathrm{CSAT}}}(x), and we read it as though it were a distribution-free L(x)L(x).

There is an argument in the exam's defense. Anyone who can read difficult criticism or academic prose has the command of syntax and inference that contracts and official documents demand, so they will handle those too. It's a plausible hypothesis. But it's an empirical claim that two abilities move together, not something that falls out of the definition. What makes a contract hard isn't only that its sentences run long. You have to know the technical concepts it turns on, and you have to work out how your own legal position shifts when several conditions apply at once. Nothing guarantees that someone who reads a film critic's prose without effort will read a lease well. And if contracts are what we want to measure, we can put contracts on the test. Routing around through some other kind of difficult writing is a choice that needs its own justification.

The fifth proxy on that earlier list, action, sharpens the point. With a contract, following the sentences isn't the job. You have to take what you've understood, apply it to your own situation, and see where your rights and exposures lie. The CSAT barely touches this. Even its application items hand the student a frame and ask them to apply it to a new case; they never ask what the student should do about their own rights and risks.

Measurement changes the distribution

So far I've assumed that PP sits out there already and the exam samples from it. Once an exam matters enough, though, the causation runs the other way too. Certain vocabulary and styles recur on the test, so students learn them, cram schools and study guides explain them, and that language ends up in front of more people than before. The encounter distribution doesn't exist independently of the exam. The exam measures the distribution and produces part of it at the same time.7

Low-frequency vocabulary on the CSAT isn't itself the problem. If reading academic prose at university takes a certain vocabulary, testing for it fits what the exam is for. The problem starts when one exam becomes the standard for literacy in general. One register's language gets certified as what a literate person ought to know, and language that developed in other registers never gets that status. This isn't to say myeongjing and jikjo are dead words. It's that the neighborhood where they live is a small one.

Legal language works the same way. Legal terminology isn't a problem for being imprecise — often it's precise exactly where precision is needed. Even so, knowing it already puts you in a better position inside the institution. Likewise, when an exam keeps making one register the measure of literacy, a difference in what language people have been around starts to read as a difference in how well they understand things at all.

What, then, should people be exposed to?

At this point the opening question has to shift. Asking which PP most objectively represents Korean isn't enough on its own. Since measuring changes PP, we also have to ask which PP we want to end up with.

That depends on what we're after. To prepare students for university, weight academic prose. To keep people from losing money, weight contracts, terms of service, and administrative documents. But widen the aim to helping different speakers of Korean understand each other and a different criterion is needed.

There I'd suggest cutting the cost of translation between groups. Korean holds different vocabularies across occupations, generations, hobbies, and internet communities. These aren't botched attempts at standard Korean so much as what each group arrived at while finding faster, more precise ways to talk about the problems it keeps running into.

That doesn't mean every precise expression should be folded into public language. What matters is how often the group using it runs into everyone else. A word that stays inside a small group, whose members rarely have to be understood by outsiders, is one nobody else needs to learn. But when groups that meet constantly keep hitting the same misunderstanding over the same expression, there's a cost worth cutting.

That cost usually falls on one side. Asking the smaller group to say it in the majority's language is the easier move. But getting used to another group's language takes exposure to it, and exposure follows resources — schooling, occupation, time — that aren't handed out evenly.

So the aim isn't to clear away one side's language and settle on a single one. Keep the specialized expressions where they're needed, but be able to unpack them for people outside. And public institutions, for their part, need to know how the language their citizens actually use is changing. What matters isn't that everyone speak alike. It's that people who speak differently can translate when they have to.

Conclusion

Go back to the formula and the point is short.

LP(x)=EA∼P[l(x,A)].L_P(x)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P}[l(x,A)].

Local literacy l(x,A)l(x,A) doesn't fix a person's literacy by itself. We also have to fix PP, which says how much each text counts, and where it matters, the weight ww. And for an exam with real reach, even PP isn't handed down from outside: what the exam measures changes what people read, which changes the PP that comes next. So a good measure of literacy can't stop at asking whether someone can read difficult texts. It has to settle which texts count, which misunderstandings it's trying to prevent, and what language it will put in front of people. Until those three are answered, what we're measuring isn't yet any determinate sense of literacy.
